\begin{abstract}
At high magnification, cellular patterns in histopathology have no fixed
orientation in the image, making rotation a relevant local symmetry for representation
learning. This work compares conventional denoising variational autoencoders (VAEs) and continuous-$\SOtwo$-equivariant
denoising variational autoencoders trained on the same unlabeled
patches extracted from ovarian cancer images. With both models frozen, reconstruction was evaluated on test patches. Afterwards, 
we evaluated the representation capability of both autoencoders in two downstream tasks: three-class tissue recognition at patch
level, and five-class whole-slide image (WSI) diagnosis via multiple-instance
learning. Reconstruction mean absolute error for the test patches was $0.0629$ versus $0.0642$ (conventional versus
equivariant), but the difference was not statistically significant. For three-class tissue recognition at the largest training budget of 5,671 labels per class, macro-F1 was $0.766$ versus $0.710$ (conventional versus equivariant), with no statistically significant difference. Finally, for WSI classification on 23 test slides, macro-F1 was
$0.389$ versus $0.483$ (conventional versus equivariant),
with no statistically significant difference.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
histopathology, variational autoencoder, rotation equivariance, $\SOtwo$,
whole-slide image classification, representation learning
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Digital histopathology converts glass slides into whole-slide images whose
dimensions commonly reach gigapixel scale. These images contain many local
tissue regions, but their diagnostic labels and detailed annotations require
specialist review. Representation learning from unlabeled patches offers a way
to use this image volume before fitting a smaller supervised model
\cite{litjens_survey_2017,song_artificial_2023,tellez_neural_2021}.

While orientation can be relevant to tissue architecture at lower
magnification, cellular patterns at high magnification have no fixed
orientation in the image. At this scale, a rotation changes the viewing
orientation of a patch while preserving its tissue identity and diagnostic
information. This rotational symmetry motivates incorporating rotation into
the model's architectural constraints. Ordinary convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) use convolutions that are equivariant to translations: translating an
input translates its feature map. Conventional CNNs usually learn rotational
responses from rotated examples or separate filters. In the $\SOtwo$ VAE,
steerable convolutions incorporate rotation symmetry through constrained
filters, introducing a rotational inductive bias into this VAE's
unsupervised representation learning
\cite{cohen_group_2016,cohen_steerable_2017,weiler_general_2019}.

Rotation-equivariant classifiers have previously been studied for
histopathology patch prediction \cite{veeling_rotation_2018}. VAEs
incorporating symmetry have also modeled object pose and learned orientation-invariant cell
representations \cite{nasiri_target_2022,burgess_orientation_2024}.
The comparison here uses patches from the UBC Ovarian Cancer Subtype
Classification and Outlier Detection (UBC-OCEAN) training resource
\cite{ubc_ocean_2023}. A conventional residual denoising VAE and a denoising VAE with
steerable kernels constrained for the continuous planar rotation group
$\SOtwo$ are trained without supervision
\cite{kingma_auto_encoding_2014,im_denoising_2017}. Both yield spatial
feature maps of size $16\times32\times32$.

This study examines two questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does the implemented rotation-equivariant VAE yield lower
    reconstruction error on unseen histopathology patches than the
    conventional VAE?
    \item Does the rotation-equivariant VAE representation yield higher
    classification macro-F1 under a shared supervised protocol: (a) for
    patch-level three-class tissue recognition from individual latent maps
    across label budgets, and (b) for WSI-level five-class diagnosis from
    bags of latent maps?
\end{enumerate}

Established steerable-kernel and field-aware operations are
implemented in a continuous-$\SOtwo$ denoising VAE trained on unlabeled
histopathology patches. Its encoder--decoder is compared with a conventional
VAE in reconstruction; their frozen spatial latent maps are then compared in
patch-level tissue recognition and WSI diagnosis with a patch classifier and
an MIL classifier, respectively. The two models share image populations,
latent dimensions, training budget and supervised assignments; uncertainty is
assessed at WSI level. Reconstruction point estimates favored the conventional
VAE, whereas tissue macro-F1 favored the equivariant representation at four of
five label budgets. WSI diagnosis did not show a statistically significant
difference between the representations.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Unsupervised Learning for Histopathology}
%%
Unsupervised learning uses unlabeled histopathology patches to learn
representations that can subsequently support supervised prediction.
Autoencoders learn these representations through reconstruction, while
downstream tasks assess whether they retain information relevant to tissue
recognition and slide-level diagnosis. Tellez \emph{et al.} used neural image
compression to learn patch representations without supervision before
predicting gigapixel image labels. Their comparison included reconstruction,
contrastive training and adversarial feature learning
\cite{tellez_neural_2021}.

VAEs that incorporate image symmetries provide related precedents beyond
histopathology. Nasiri and Bepler's TARGET-VAE uses a translation- and
rotation-equivariant encoder and a spatially equivariant generator to separate
object pose from a semantic representation \cite{nasiri_target_2022}.
Burgess \emph{et al.} introduced O2-VAE for cell and organelle shape profiling.
It constructs orientation-invariant latent representations using equivariant
encoder operations and pooling, and aligns the reconstruction with the input
\cite{burgess_orientation_2024}.

These studies motivate learning features without supervision and assessing
their relevance through downstream tasks. Here, the VAEs are trained on
unlabeled histopathology patches, evaluated in reconstruction, and frozen
before their representations are used for patch-level tissue recognition and
WSI-level diagnosis.

\subsection{Rotation Equivariance}

Ordinary CNNs build on translation-equivariant convolutions: shifting the
input shifts the resulting feature map. Group-equivariant convolutional
networks generalize this construction
to other transformations, including rotations, by requiring layers to satisfy
a prescribed transformation law for a selected group \cite{cohen_group_2016}.
\autoref{fig:rotation-equivariance} illustrates this relation: applying a
rotation before an equivariant mapping corresponds to applying the prescribed
rotation to its output. For vector-valued fields, this output transformation
rotates both spatial locations and vector components.
Steerable convolutions extend the construction to continuous rotations by
restricting the kernel space according to the representations carried by the
input and output fields \cite{cohen_steerable_2017,weiler_general_2019}.
When the prediction task shares this symmetry, the restriction can reduce
the set of functions the network can represent \cite{elesedy_provably_2021}.
The architecture also changes parameter count and computational work; both
are reported alongside model performance. Previous histopathology work
evaluated rotation equivariance in supervised patch classifiers
\cite{veeling_rotation_2018,graham_dense_2020}.

\subsection{Multi-Instance Learning}

Whole-slide images can contain billions of pixels. Their size makes processing
at native resolution in a single forward pass computationally impractical. A common approach therefore
divides each WSI into patches, learns a local representation, and aggregates
patch features for slide-level prediction. Attention-based multiple
instance learning (MIL) provides a related supervised formulation in which a
WSI is treated as a bag of instances and only the bag label is required
\cite{ilse_attention_2018}. CLAM combines attention pooling with instance-level
clustering \cite{lu_data_2021}. DSMIL combines contrastive patch representation
learning with dual-stream aggregation \cite{li_dualstream_2021}, whereas
TransMIL models relations among patch instances during aggregation
\cite{shao_transmil_2021}. Here, MIL tests the frozen patch representations under the same WSI
classification protocol. \autoref{fig:mil-concept} illustrates the slide-to-bag
formulation.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{UBC-OCEAN dataset}

The UBC-OCEAN ovarian-cancer training resource contains 538 slide images:
513 conventional whole-slide images (WSIs) and 25 tissue microarray (TMA)
images \cite{ubc_ocean_2023}. A WSI shows a large tissue section; a TMA shows
multiple small tissue cores in one image. The resource describes WSIs as
large, approximately $20\times$ scans and TMAs as smaller, approximately
$4000\times4000$-pixel, $40\times$ images. This scale and layout difference
motivated using only the 513 non-TMA WSIs in the present experiment.
\autoref{fig:dataset-examples} shows a representative image of each type and their
original pixel dimensions.
For an image of width $W$ and height $H$, a non-overlapping $256$-pixel grid
contains approximately $\lfloor W/256\rfloor\lfloor H/256\rfloor$ full
patches before foreground filtering. At the dimensions quoted by the resource,
a $4000\times4000$ image yields about 225 full patches, whereas a
$100000\times50000$ image yields about 76,000; the latter is an illustrative
large-WSI scale, not a count for an individual study slide. The
resource also provides 152 supplemental color masks paired with a subset of
those WSIs. These masks mark selected tumor, stroma and necrosis regions;
they do not annotate every tissue pixel. The other 361 WSIs have no
supplemental mask. The diagnostic label of each image is one of clear cell
carcinoma (CC), endometrioid carcinoma (EC), high-grade serous carcinoma
(HGSC), low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC), or mucinous carcinoma (MC).

Each WSI was divided into non-overlapping RGB $256\times256$-pixel patches.
A full WSI has one diagnosis label, so thousands of patches extracted from
that slide share the same slide-level label. Local tissue labels, in contrast,
come only from eligible regions of the supplemental masks. Splits were made
by WSI: no patches from one slide crossed train, validation or test
boundaries.

\autoref{fig:dataset-examples} shows two full-resolution patches beside their
source WSI thumbnail, a partial supplemental mask on a second WSI, and a TMA.
The thumbnail makes the slide-to-patch scale change visible; model inference
used crops from the full-resolution WSI. The mask marks selected tissue regions
rather than the full image.

\subsection{Patch Selection and Tissue Annotations}

Foreground selection for reconstruction and WSI diagnosis was derived from
the image rather than from human masks. Each WSI thumbnail was
converted to HSV, and Otsu's threshold was applied to the saturation channel
\cite{otsu_threshold_1979}. A full-resolution grid cell was retained when more
than $60\%$ of its projected thumbnail area belonged to foreground. The resulting coordinates were used for VAE development, reconstruction and
WSI diagnosis without consulting the supplemental tissue masks.

Supplemental masks were used only to construct the tissue-recognition
population. Red, green and blue mask regions were interpreted as tumor,
stroma and necrosis. The masks are not exhaustive; an unpainted region cannot
be treated as a negative class. A patch was therefore eligible for tissue
classification only when at least $10\%$ of its area was annotated and at least
$95\%$ of that annotated area belonged to one tissue class. The resulting task
classifies high-purity annotated regions and does not constitute dense
pixel-level segmentation of the complete WSI.

\subsection{Data Populations and Partitions}

The 513 eligible WSIs were partitioned at WSI level into three disjoint
subsets: 361 for unsupervised VAE development, 129 for supervised classifier
development, and 23 for a shared sealed test (\autoref{fig:data-split}). VAE
development used 322 training and 39 validation WSIs, contributing 300,000 and
30,000 unlabeled patches. Patch sampling supplied 60,000 training and 6,000
validation patches from each WSI diagnosis; diagnosis was not a VAE training
target. WSI counts were not balanced by diagnosis
(\autoref{tab:task-populations}). Supervised development used 106 training and
23 validation WSIs. Together with the shared test, these formed the 152-WSI
cohort with supplemental masks. These assignments were fixed before VAE or
classifier outputs on that cohort were inspected. They were shared by both
downstream tasks and used diagnosis and annotated-tissue availability, but
not image pixels, VAE representations or model predictions. The same 23 test
WSIs were also used for reconstruction evaluation.

For WSI diagnosis, each slide was one labeled MIL bag containing all its
foreground patches selected by Otsu thresholding. Each complete WSI contributed
one diagnosis label shared by all patches in its bag. The patch-level tissue classifier
instead used high-purity annotated tumor, stroma and necrosis patches. Its
training subsets were balanced at the five nested label budgets introduced
above, whereas validation
and test used fixed, unbalanced patch sets. \autoref{tab:task-populations}
separates the inputs and class counts of these three stages.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

The same frozen VAE pair was evaluated in three tasks. First, reconstruction
measured whether each encoder--decoder recovered clean, unseen patches.
Second, a patch classifier used frozen latent maps to predict local tissue
type. It was trained at five nested label budgets, with the same validation
and test sets at every budget. Third, a multiple-instance learning (MIL)
classifier aggregated patch embeddings to predict one diagnosis per WSI.

The VAEs were trained on unlabeled patches and frozen before classification.
Reconstruction used each encoder and decoder; classification used only the
deterministic posterior mean. Both VAEs received the same ordered patches,
normalization, corruption policy, loss, update count and validation schedule.
Downstream pairs shared WSI assignments, patch coordinates, classifier
initialization, epoch order and model-selection rule. The architectures differ
in parameter count and computational cost (\autoref{tab:architectures}).

\subsection{Variational Autoencoders and Shared Architecture}

An autoencoder learns to turn an image into a smaller representation and then
reconstruct the image from it. Its \emph{encoder} extracts and compresses visual
features, and its \emph{decoder} uses the compressed embedding to reproduce
the input. In a VAE, the encoder specifies a distribution over embeddings
rather than one fixed code: here a diagonal Gaussian with mean
$\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and log-variance $\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2$. During
training, a latent sample $\mathbf{z}$ is drawn by reparameterization
\cite{kingma_auto_encoding_2014}. The distribution is regularized toward a
unit Gaussian while the decoder learns image reconstruction. After training,
the posterior mean is a deterministic feature map for the classifiers. For
reconstruction evaluation, the decoder reconstructs the clean patch from
that mean. \autoref{fig:vae-pipeline} shows these paths.

Both models used a four-stage residual encoder informed by ResNet-18
\cite{he_deep_2016}: a stem and two residual blocks at each stage, with
$2\times$ fixed anti-aliased downsampling between stages. Their decoder used
two residual blocks per stage and reversed the three resolution changes with
bilinear upsampling followed by convolution. The spatial sequence was
$256\rightarrow128\rightarrow64\rightarrow32$ pixels per side in the
encoder and reversed in the decoder. Separate posterior heads produced
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mu},\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times32\times32}.
\end{equation}
The decoder returned a raw three-channel image from a zero-initialized
output convolution, without a final $\tanh$. The 16-channel spatial embedding
preserves coarse locations within the
patch; its $32\times32$ grid is smaller than the original $256\times256$
input. \autoref{tab:architectures} compares the two implementations.

\subsection{Continuous Rotation Equivariance}

A \emph{group} is a collection of transformations that can be composed and
inverted. The planar rotation group $\SOtwo$ contains every angle from
$0$ to $360$ degrees, with angle addition as its composition rule. A map
$f$ is \emph{equivariant} when transforming its input also transforms its
output in a predictable way:
\begin{equation}
f(T_g\mathbf{x})=\rho(g)f(\mathbf{x}),\qquad g\in\SOtwo.
\label{eq:equivariance}
\end{equation}
Here $T_g$ rotates an image and $\rho(g)$ specifies how the output feature
field must rotate. This is distinct from \emph{invariance}, in which the
output stays identical after rotation. For a scalar image feature, spatial
locations rotate but the value at a corresponding location does not; for a
vector feature, its two components rotate as well.

The conventional VAE used ordinary convolutions, GroupNorm and learned scalar
gates. Its hidden channel counts were 32, 48, 64 and 96. The experimental
$\SOtwo$ VAE replaced the convolutional layers with steerable convolutions
\cite{cohen_steerable_2017,weiler_general_2019}. A steerable kernel is a
filter whose rotated version follows a prescribed rule: rather than learning
an unrelated filter for every orientation, the network learns coefficients
of radial and angular basis functions that obey the input/output field
transformation laws. This construction constrains the convolutional
operators; it does not by itself establish that the complete trained encoder
obeys \autoref{eq:equivariance} exactly under sampled image rotations.

Its hidden features contained $F_0$ scalar fields and $F_1$ vector fields.
An $F_0$ copy stores one value per pixel and changes only its spatial
location under a rotation. An $F_1$ copy stores an ordered pair of values per
pixel; that pair rotates by the same angle as the image coordinate system.
Thus, a layout with $n_0$ scalar copies and $n_1$ vector copies has
$n_0+2n_1$ numerical channels, not $n_0+n_1$. The four stages used
$(n_0,n_1)=(16,16),(24,24),(32,32),(48,48)$. Field-aware normalization
and gates based on the $F_1$ vector magnitude were used so these operations
respect the field types. The precise nonlinearities and normalizations are
given below. The posterior heads returned 16 $F_0$ fields and
no $F_1$ fields, matching the conventional $16\times32\times32$ posterior.
\subsubsection{Kernel Support and Learned Parameterization}
\label{sec:kernel-support}
Comparing conventional and rotation-equivariant CNNs is not straightforward:
matching kernel support, learned parameter count and computational cost can
require different architectural choices. The conventional VAE uses
$5\times5$ residual kernels, whereas the $\SOtwo$ VAE uses $7\times7$
steerable kernels. This difference follows from the spatial sampling
requirements of the selected steerable basis.

The basis combines angular harmonics with Gaussian radial profiles, a common
construction in steerable CNNs \cite{weiler_general_2019}. The radial
envelopes are centered at approximately $1.00$, $1.90$ and $2.75$ pixels, each with
a standard deviation of $0.30$ pixels. Its $F_1$-to-$F_1$ terms include a second
angular harmonic, corresponding to two cycles around the filter center.
A centered $5\times5$ grid extends only two pixels along each coordinate axis;
only its four corners lie beyond radius $2.5$ pixels. Consequently, the outer
radial envelope is sampled unevenly across angles. The $7\times7$ grid
extends three pixels along each axis and provides additional samples near
this envelope, improving its angular coverage and reducing the risk of
discretization aliasing.

While the larger grid might appear to give the steerable layers an advantage,
their filters remain constrained to a fixed steerable basis. They learn
coefficients of this basis rather than an independent weight at every kernel
location. For example, the steerable convolution in the first same-resolution
residual stage uses only $30.0\%$ as many learned parameters as its conventional
counterpart: 7,680 basis coefficients versus 25,600 free weights. Across the
complete VAEs, the $\SOtwo$ model has $29.8\%$ as many learned parameters,
although its estimated convolutional work is higher
(\autoref{tab:architectures}).

Larger steerable than conventional kernels have also been used in earlier
comparisons. Weiler and Cesa used larger steerable kernels to mitigate
discretization artifacts while adjusting feature widths to preserve the total
parameter count \cite{weiler_general_2019}. Worrall \emph{et al.} compared
$5\times5$ harmonic filters with $3\times3$ conventional filters and
reported their different parameter counts \cite{worrall_harmonic_2017}.
\autoref{fig:residual-blocks} shows the two residual building blocks before
\autoref{fig:vae-architectures} assembles them into the full encoder and
decoder schedules.

\subsection{Field Gates and Normalization}

The conventional VAE applies a learned scalar gate to each hidden channel,
including after residual addition. This gate is a parameterized variant of
the sigmoid-weighted linear unit (SiLU), $u\,\sigma(u)$
\cite{elfwing_silu_2018}, with a learned slope and offset inside the sigmoid.
For a channel value $u_c$,
\begin{equation}
A_0(u_c)=u_c\,\sigma(a_cu_c+b_c),\qquad
\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}},
\label{eq:scalar-gate}
\end{equation}
where $a_c$ and $b_c$ are learned per-channel parameters, initialized to
$a_c=1$ and $b_c=0$, so the initial activation is SiLU. The $F_0$ fields in
the $\SOtwo$ VAE use the same rule with one parameter pair per scalar field.
For an $F_1$ field $\mathbf v_j=(v_{j,1},v_{j,2})$, the equivariant VAE gates
both components by the same factor computed from their rotation-invariant
magnitude:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_j&=\sqrt{v_{j,1}^{2}+v_{j,2}^{2}+10^{-4}},\\
A_1(\mathbf v_j)&=\mathbf v_j\,\sigma(\alpha_j r_j+\beta_j).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radial-gate}
\end{equation}
The learned $\alpha_j,\beta_j$ are shared by the two components. A planar
rotation changes the vector direction but not $r_j$, so this gate rotates its
output together with the input vector.

Before these gates, the conventional network uses affine GroupNorm with
eight groups. For each sample and channel $c$, it computes the mean and
variance over the channels in $c$'s group and all spatial positions:
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{GN}}(u_c)=\gamma_c
\frac{u_c-\mu_{G(c)}}{\sqrt{s^2_{G(c)}+10^{-5}}}+\delta_c.
\label{eq:group-norm}
\end{equation}
The equivariant network applies this same eight-group formula only to its
$F_0$ scalar fields. Its $F_1$ vectors instead use four groups and a shared
root-mean-square denominator for both components:
\begin{equation}
N_1(\mathbf v_j)=\gamma_j
\frac{\mathbf v_j}{\sqrt{
\underset{k\in G(j),\,d\in\{1,2\},\,p}{\operatorname{mean}}
 v_{k,d,p}^{2}+10^{-5}}}.
\label{eq:vector-norm}
\end{equation}
Here $p$ indexes spatial positions within one sample. The vector rule has
no mean subtraction or additive bias, because either would introduce a
preferred direction. Its denominator is unchanged by rotations in feature
space. The normalization statistics and gates are evaluated in FP32 in the
$\SOtwo$ implementation.

\subsection{VAE Objective and Training}

Both models used a denoising VAE training criterion: an encoder sees a
possibly corrupted patch, and the decoder is trained to reconstruct the
corresponding clean patch \cite{im_denoising_2017}. In the implemented training procedure,
with probability $0.3$ the corruption operator $C$ perturbed
hematoxylin/eosin/residual stain coordinates and added image-space Gaussian
noise
\cite{ruifrok_color_2001}. The resulting input was clamped to the
normalized $[-1,1]$ RGB domain. A patch could therefore be clean or
corrupted at the input, while its reconstruction target was always the same
clean patch:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}\xrightarrow{\ C\ }\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
\xrightarrow{\ q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ }\mathbf{z}
\xrightarrow{\ D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ }\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
\quad\text{compared with }\mathbf{x}.
\label{eq:denoise-path}
\end{equation}
The encoder distribution
$q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})$ is the
diagonal Gaussian determined by the mean and log-variance heads;
$D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is the decoder. During training,
$\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$,
with $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$.
The implemented loss was
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}={}&\operatorname{MAE}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x})
+0.1[1-\operatorname{SSIM}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x})]\\
&+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:vae-loss}
\end{equation}
where MAE measures pixelwise reconstruction error, SSIM measures structural
similarity \cite{wang_image_2004}, and the KL term regularizes the
input-dependent posterior toward a unit Gaussian. The KL weight increased
linearly from zero to $0.01$ during the first 6,000 updates and remained fixed
thereafter.

AdamW \cite{loshchilov_adamw_2019} used coefficients $(0.9,0.999)$,
$\epsilon=10^{-8}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$ and gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$.
The learning rate increased linearly for 600 updates to $10^{-3}$ and then
decreased with a cosine schedule to $10^{-5}$. Each model was trained for
60,000 successful updates with a global batch of 50 patches distributed over
two NVIDIA T4 GPUs. Validation and checkpointing occurred every 3,000 updates.
The comparison used the final update-60,000 checkpoint for both models rather
than selecting different VAE checkpoints from the downstream results.

After VAE training, all encoder and decoder parameters were frozen. Downstream
tasks used the posterior mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ rather than a sampled latent.
For every selected coordinate, both encoders received the same RGB patch. The
resulting paired latent datasets had identical WSI membership, tissue labels
and patch coordinates.

\subsection{Reconstruction Evaluation}

The reconstruction test contained 67,138 foreground patches from the 23 test
WSIs. For each slide, eligible foreground coordinates were sorted by row and
column ($y$, then $x$). If there were more than 3,000, 3,000 evenly spaced
rank positions were selected across that ordered list; otherwise all were
retained. Thus, 22 slides contributed 3,000 patches each and one contributed
its available 1,138. The cap limited the influence of slides with many more
eligible patches.
MAE and mean squared error (MSE) were measured in the normalized $[-1,1]$
domain. Peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and SSIM were measured after
conversion to $[0,1]$ and clamping. Mean MAE was the primary reconstruction
measure. The other metrics describe complementary error properties.

\subsection{Classification A: Tissue Recognition and Label Budgets}

Tissue classification uses one patch at a time, without combining
information across a WSI. It tests how much tissue information each frozen
representation retains as the number of labeled training patches changes.
The task predicted tumor, stroma or necrosis from a single latent map. The classifier contained three convolutional stages
$16\rightarrow32\rightarrow64\rightarrow128$, GroupNorm, GELU activations
\cite{hendrycks_gelu_2016}, adaptive average pooling and a linear
$128\rightarrow3$ output layer.

Training subsets contained 250, 500, 1,000, 2,500 and 5,671 labeled patches
per class. They were class balanced, nested and selected without replacement;
every smaller set was a subset of each larger one. WSI-stratified ordering
ensured that the smallest subset did not collapse onto a single slide. Each
normal/equivariant pair started from identical classifier weights and received
the same patch order. Models were optimized for at most 30 epochs, and the
best validation macro-F1 checkpoint was retained. The test distribution was
not balanced and contained 21,796 tumor, 8,500 stroma and 1,276 necrosis
patches from 23 WSIs.

\subsection{Classification B: Five-Class WSI Diagnosis}

The diagnostic task predicted the five ovarian-cancer subtypes. Every selected
foreground patch was represented by its frozen posterior mean. A trainable
convolutional patch encoder compressed the $16\times32\times32$ latent map.
Two local attention blocks exchanged information within a radius-two spatial
neighborhood, and a global stream summarized the complete WSI through learned
sigmoid attention. A final classification layer produced five logits. The
supervised model contained approximately 1.51 million parameters.

Training used one complete WSI bag per optimizer step, AdamW and class-weighted
cross-entropy computed from the 106 training WSIs. Each representation branch
started from the same classifier weights and received the same WSI order. The
maximum was 150 epochs, validation was performed twice per epoch, and each
branch independently retained its highest validation macro-F1 checkpoint.
The primary test metric was macro-F1; balanced accuracy, accuracy, mean
cross-entropy, classwise F1 and confusion matrices were secondary summaries.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

All difference intervals are reported as conventional-minus-equivariant.
Reconstruction differences used 10,000 paired cluster-bootstrap replicates at
WSI level \cite{field_bootstrapping_2007}. WSI diagnosis used 10,000 paired
bootstrap replicates stratified by diagnosis. Tissue recognition used a paired
WSI bootstrap stratified by observed tissue support. The five label-budget
macro-F1 differences and the area under the log-budget learning curve (AULC)
difference were considered together as six related comparisons. Their $95\%$
intervals were adjusted simultaneously: across the six comparisons, the
bootstrap procedure targets $95\%$ coverage for the whole set of differences.
A difference interval entirely below zero favors the equivariant model when
higher is better; an interval entirely above zero favors the conventional
model. An interval containing zero does not establish a statistically
significant difference at the $5\%$ level, nor does it establish equivalence.
Patch standard deviations describe variation among patches, while confidence
intervals resample WSIs as the biological units.

\section{Discussion}

Which representation scored higher depended on the task and the number of
labeled tissue patches. The conventional VAE had lower pooled MAE/MSE
and higher pooled PSNR/SSIM point estimates. The equivariant representation had higher tissue
macro-F1 at the four smaller budgets and a higher WSI macro-F1 point estimate.
Most confidence intervals for the main comparisons included zero. The
single tissue-budget interval that excluded zero applies to that contrast only
and does not establish a monotone or general label-efficiency effect. The
model with lower reconstruction error did not consistently achieve higher
classification macro-F1.

Patch-based WSI systems depend on both the instance representation and the bag
aggregator. Prior MIL work has developed the representation, aggregation or
both \cite{ilse_attention_2018,lu_data_2021,li_dualstream_2021,shao_transmil_2021}.
This experiment held the downstream protocol fixed while changing the frozen
representation. The WSI interval remained wide, and classwise estimates were
unstable for diagnoses with two test slides. The results characterize the two
implemented representation branches but do not establish a general WSI or
tissue-classification advantage.

The models also differ in capacity and computation. Graham \emph{et al.}
matched parameter counts in comparisons of rotation-equivariant histology
classifiers \cite{graham_dense_2020}; these two VAEs were not
parameter-matched. The equivariant VAE uses $29.8\%$ as many learned parameters
but requires more estimated multiply-accumulate operations. Any performance
difference may reflect equivariance, parameter count, optimization or their
interaction. The experiment does not separate these mechanisms.

\section{Limitations}

Each VAE and downstream configuration was trained once. The bootstrap
intervals quantify variation across the observed WSIs, not variation across
new initializations, data orders or optimization runs. The WSI test contains 23 slides, including two each for LGSC
and MC.
Tissue recognition contains many patches but only 23 WSIs, and
necrosis is present in five of them.

Supplemental tissue masks are incomplete. The tissue experiment evaluates
selected high-purity annotated regions and cannot be interpreted as exhaustive
segmentation of complete WSIs. The study uses one ovarian-cancer data source.

\section{Conclusion}

This study compared conventional and continuous-$\SOtwo$ VAEs as frozen
histopathology representations under a common experimental protocol. With one
training run per configuration and intervals limited to WSI resampling, the results varied by task and label budget. The experiment did not
establish a general advantage for either implemented representation; repeated
training runs and models with matched parameter counts are needed to isolate
the contribution of continuous rotation equivariance.
